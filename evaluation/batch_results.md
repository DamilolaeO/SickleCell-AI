## 55. What does HbSS mean?

*Category:* definitions | *Expected:* answer | *Got:* answer | **PASS**

HbSS is the laboratory shorthand for *homozygous sickle‑cell disease*, meaning a person has the sickle‑cell mutation on both copies of the beta‑globin gene [1]. ## 56. What is HbSC?

*Category:* definitions | *Expected:* answer | *Got:* answer | **PASS**

**What is HbSC?**  
HbSC is a particular type of sickle‑cell disease that occurs when a person inherits one copy of the *HbS* gene (the sickle‑cell variant) and one copy of the *HbC* gene (a different abnormal form of haemoglobin). In this combination, the red blood cells contain both abnormal haemoglobin molecules. 

- **HbS** (haemoglobin S) is the classic sickle‑cell variant that can cause red cells to bend into a “sickle” shape, especially when oxygen levels drop. This shape can block small blood vessels and lead to pain and other complications. [1]  
- **HbC** (haemoglobin C) is another abnormal haemoglobin that can also affect red‑cell shape, but it usually causes milder problems on its own. [1]  

When the two are together (HbSC), the disease tends to be milder than the classic HbSS form, but people can still experience pain crises, infections and other sickle‑cell complications. The severity varies from person to person. 

If you’re wondering how HbSC might affect you or a family member, it’s best to discuss it with your haematology team or GP, who can explain the specific risks and management plan for your situation.

## 57. What is the difference between SCD and SCT?

*Category:* definitions | *Expected:* answer | *Got:* answer | **PASS**

Sickle cell disease (SCD) occurs when a person inherits two copies of the abnormal haemoglobin S gene, one from each parent, which causes red blood cells to become stiff and sickle‑shaped, leading to painful crises, infections and organ damage that usually start in early childhood [1][2]. 
Sickle cell trait (SCT) is when only one copy of the haemoglobin S gene is present and the other is normal; most people with SCT feel completely normal and do not develop the painful or organ‑damaging episodes seen in SCD [1][2]. 
Because SCT carries only one abnormal gene, it is usually asymptomatic but can be passed on to children; if a child inherits two abnormal genes they will have SCD [1][2]. 
SCT can also provide some protection against malaria in certain regions, which has influenced its distribution in malaria‑endemic areas [3]. 
In contrast, individuals with SCD are more susceptible to malaria and its complications because they have two abnormal genes [3].

## 58. What is a sickle cell crisis?

*Category:* definitions | *Expected:* answer | *Got:* answer | **PASS**

A sickle cell crisis is a sudden, severe flare‑up that can happen to anyone with sickle cell disease. During a crisis, the red blood cells – which are normally round – become stiff and “sickle”‑shaped. These misshapen cells can block small blood vessels, cutting off oxygen to parts of the body. The blockage causes intense pain and can affect many organs, leading to symptoms such as:

* Very painful episodes in bones, joints, muscles or the chest  
* Blurred vision or eye problems  
* A high temperature (fever)  
* A painful erection that does not go away (called priapism)  

The crisis can last from a few hours to several weeks and may be triggered by dehydration, a sudden change in temperature, stress or other factors that make the blood thicker or slower to flow [1]. 

If you or someone you care for is experiencing severe pain, breathing difficulties, a high temperature, or a painful erection, it is important to seek medical help right away. Call emergency services (999 in the UK) and let them know you suspect a sickle cell crisis; they will guide you on what to do next [1]. 

If you have any concerns about how to manage or recognise a crisis, please discuss them with your haematology team or GP.

  - you have an erection that is painful or does not go away

## 59. What is acute chest syndrome?

*Category:* definitions | *Expected:* answer | *Got:* answer | **PASS**

Acute chest syndrome (ACS) is a serious complication that can happen in people with sickle cell disease. It is characterised by a new patch of lung inflammation seen on a chest X‑ray together with symptoms such as chest pain, rapid breathing, wheezing, cough or a fever above 38.5 °C [1]. In children, ACS is the second most common reason for hospital admission and the most common cause of death in sickle‑cell patients, although children usually have a milder course than adults [1].

The exact cause of ACS is complex. It can arise from blocked blood vessels in the lungs, tiny lung infarcts, inflammation, poor ventilation, or infections that are common in children with sickle cell disease [1]. Because it can progress quickly to breathing difficulties, doctors look for signs such as abnormal breathing, low oxygen levels (≤95 %) or a need for extra oxygen, and they monitor patients closely from the moment they arrive until they leave the hospital [2].

If you or someone you know has sudden chest pain, fever, or breathing problems, it is important to seek urgent medical help. Talk to your haematology team or GP about what to do if you suspect ACS.

## 62. What is sickle cell trait?

*Category:* definitions | *Expected:* answer | *Got:* answer | **PASS**

Sickle cell trait (SCT) is a genetic condition that occurs when a person inherits one normal haemoglobin gene (HbA) and one sickle‑cell gene (HbS). Because only one copy of the abnormal gene is present, the red blood cells are usually normal in shape and function, so most people with SCT do not experience the pain, infections or other problems that people with sickle cell disease (SCD) do [1][2]. 

SCT is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern: each parent of a child with SCD carries one copy of the HbS gene, but they themselves are usually symptom‑free. If both parents carry the trait, there is a 25 % chance that their child will inherit two copies and develop SCD, a 50 % chance of inheriting one copy (SCT), and a 25 % chance of inheriting two normal genes [2]. 

People with SCT can pass the HbS gene to their children, so knowing your carrier status is useful for family planning. While SCT is generally harmless, it can sometimes cause complications in extreme situations, such as high altitude or intense physical exertion, but these are rare [1]. 

If you’re unsure whether you carry the trait or have questions about your family’s genetic risk, talk to your haematology team or GP.

## 63. What does hydroxycarbamide do?

*Category:* medicines | *Expected:* answer | *Got:* answer | **PASS**

Hydroxycarbamide (also called hydroxyurea) is one of the main medicines used to help people with sickle‑cell disease (SCD). It works in a few different ways:

* **Boosts “good” blood cells** – it encourages the body to make more fetal haemoglobin, a type of haemoglobin that does not sickle. This lowers the amount of the sickle‑shaped haemoglobin (HbS) that can block blood flow. 
* **Reduces inflammation and sticking of cells** – it lowers white‑blood‑cell numbers and the molecules on the surface of red cells that help them stick to blood‑ vessel walls. This can lessen the chances of painful blockages (vaso‑occlusive crises). 
* **Cuts down serious complications** – studies show that hydroxyurea can reduce the risk of acute chest syndrome, a life‑threatening lung problem that is common in SCD, and may improve overall survival in children [1]. 

In everyday care, doctors give hydroxycarbamide to most people with SCD who are well enough, together with other treatments such as pain relief, antibiotics to prevent infections, and sometimes blood transfusions or stem‑cell transplants when needed [2].

If you’re wondering whether hydroxycarbamide is right for you or how it might fit into your treatment plan, please discuss it with your haematology team or GP.

## 65. Why do people with sickle cell take folic acid?

*Category:* medicines | *Expected:* answer | *Got:* answer | **PASS**

People with sickle cell disease (SCD) lose red blood cells more quickly than normal because the cells are fragile and break apart (haemolysis). The body therefore needs to make new red cells all the time. Folic acid (vitamin B9) is a key nutrient for building the DNA that new red cells need, so a deficiency can worsen anaemia and fatigue. Because of this increased demand, doctors often give people with SCD a daily folic‑acid supplement to help keep their blood healthy and to minimise the risk of a folate shortfall [1]. 

Research also shows that while folic‑acid supplementation can raise blood folate levels, its effect on improving anaemia or other symptoms is still unclear, and it may mask some blood‑test abnormalities that could indicate other problems such as vitamin B12 deficiency [1]. 

In short, folic acid is taken mainly to support the rapid production of new red blood cells that sickle‑cell patients need to replace those that break down quickly. 

If you’re unsure whether you should be taking folic acid or how much, it’s best to discuss this with your haematology team or GP.

## 67. What is gene therapy for sickle cell and who can get it?

*Category:* medicines | *Expected:* answer | *Got:* answer | **PASS**

Gene therapy for sickle cell disease is a cutting‑edge treatment that tries to fix the underlying genetic fault that causes the abnormal red blood cells. In most trials, doctors take a patient’s own stem cells (the cells that make blood) and use a tool called CRISPR‑Cas9 or a viral vector to change the faulty β‑globin gene so it produces normal haemoglobin instead of the sickle‑shaped protein. The edited stem cells are then returned to the patient, where they grow into healthy blood cells that no longer sickle. This approach can reduce or even stop painful crises and other complications, acting as a potential long‑term cure or remission [1].

Who can get it? 
* The treatment is still experimental and is usually offered only in specialised centres that run clinical trials. 
* Eligibility depends on the type of sickle cell disease, the patient’s overall health, and whether the benefits outweigh the risks. 
* In many countries, only patients who meet strict criteria—such as having a suitable donor for stem‑cell transplantation or being able to undergo the gene‑editing procedure—are considered. 
* In high‑resource settings, some patients have already received gene‑edited stem‑cell therapy, but the cost and need for specialised equipment mean it is not yet widely available, especially in low‑ and middle‑income countries [2].

If you or a family member has sickle cell disease and you’re curious about gene therapy, it’s best to discuss it with your haematology team or GP. They can explain whether you meet the criteria for a clinical trial or other treatment options.

## 76. Can I have a baby if I have sickle cell?

*Category:* pregnancy | *Expected:* answer | *Got:* answer | **PASS**

If you have sickle cell disease (SCD) you can still have children, but the chance that a baby will also have SCD depends on your partner’s blood‑cell genes. 

* **Both parents have SCD (HbSS)** – every child will inherit two abnormal genes and will have SCD. 
* **You have SCD and your partner is a carrier (HbAS)** – each pregnancy gives a 50 % chance that the baby will have SCD and a 50 % chance that the baby will be a carrier. 
* **You have SCD and your partner is normal (HbAA)** – all babies will be carriers but will not have SCD.

## 80. Why does cold weather cause a crisis?

*Category:* daily_life | *Expected:* answer | *Got:* answer | **PASS**

Cold weather can trigger a sickle‑cell crisis because the body’s blood vessels react to the drop in temperature. When it’s chilly, the vessels in your limbs and other parts of the body tighten (a process called vasoconstriction). This makes the blood thicker and slows its flow. In people with sickle‑cell disease, the red blood cells are already stiff and can “sickle” when they’re under stress. The slowed, thicker blood can cause these sickled cells to stick together and block the tiny blood vessels that supply oxygen to tissues. When oxygen can’t reach parts of the body, pain and other crisis symptoms appear. Sudden changes in temperature, such as moving from a warm indoor space to a cold outdoor area, are a recognised trigger for crises [1][2]. 

If you or a loved one experiences sudden, severe pain, breathing trouble, or a high temperature during cold weather, seek urgent medical help. It’s best to talk to your haematology team or GP about ways to stay warm and avoid triggers, and to discuss any preventive measures that might help you manage the condition.

  - you have an erection that is painful or does not go away

## 83. Can stress trigger a crisis?

## 85. Why do fevers matter in sickle cell?

*Category:* daily_life | *Expected:* answer | *Got:* answer | **PASS**

Fevers are a red flag in sickle‑cell disease because they often signal an infection or a state that can trigger a crisis. When the body is hot, it needs more oxygen and fluids. If you’re already carrying sickled red cells, the extra demand can make them stick together and block tiny blood vessels – a process called vaso‑occlusion. This can cause sudden, severe pain, swelling, or even organ damage. 

Infections are especially dangerous. Research shows that respiratory pathogens can lead to a serious complication called acute chest syndrome (ACS), which is more severe in people with sickle‑cell disease than in the general child population. ACS can develop even if the infection seems mild, because the sickled cells and the inflammation they cause make the lungs more vulnerable [1]. 

High temperatures can also cause dehydration, which thickens the blood and further promotes sickling. Because of these risks, a fever in someone with sickle‑cell disease is treated as a potential emergency and should prompt immediate medical attention [2]. 

If you or a loved one develops a fever, especially with pain, breathing trouble, or a high temperature, contact your haematology team or GP right away. They can assess whether you need urgent care to prevent a crisis.

## 87. Do herbal remedies work for sickle cell?

*Category:* tricky | *Expected:* answer | *Got:* answer | **PASS**

Herbal remedies are being studied as possible helpers for people with sickle cell disease, but the evidence is still early and not yet proven for everyday use. 

A systematic review of plant‑based therapies looked at many studies that tested herbs in laboratory settings and in animal models. The review found that several plants, such as *Carica papaya* and *Garcinia kola*, showed “antisickling” activity – meaning they could reduce the tendency of red blood cells to harden and block blood vessels. However, the research is largely experimental and has not yet been confirmed in large, well‑controlled human trials. The authors call for more standardised research before any firm conclusions can be drawn. [1]  

Because the evidence is still preliminary, most clinical guidelines do not recommend herbal remedies as a replacement for proven treatments such as hydroxyurea, pain management, or red‑cell exchange. If you’re curious about herbal options, it’s best to discuss them with your haematology team or GP, who can help you weigh the potential benefits against the lack of robust evidence and any possible interactions with your current medicines.

## 89. What is avascular necrosis?

*Category:* tricky | *Expected:* answer | *Got:* answer | **PASS**

Avascular necrosis (AVN) is a condition where a part of bone dies because it no longer receives enough blood. Blood carries oxygen and nutrients that keep bone alive; when the supply is cut off, the bone tissue can collapse and become painful. In people with sickle cell disease (SCD) the most common site is the head of the femur (the ball of the hip joint) – this is called osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) [1]. 

AVN usually follows a painful “crisis” or blockage of blood flow in the tiny vessels that feed the bone. Repeated episodes can damage the bone’s micro‑circulation, leading to the death of bone cells and eventual collapse of the joint surface. The result is joint pain, stiffness and, if untreated, loss of function. 

Because AVN can develop gradually, it is important to recognise early signs such as persistent hip or knee pain that does not improve with rest. If you or a family member notice such symptoms, it is best to speak with your haematology team or GP for assessment and possible imaging to confirm the diagnosis. Early treatment can help preserve joint health and reduce pain.

## 91. Can men with sickle cell have children?

*Category:* tricky | *Expected:* answer | *Got:* answer | **PASS**

Men with sickle cell disease can often have children, but the condition can affect fertility. Research shows that the repeated blood‑clotting and low‑oxygen episodes that occur in sickle cell can damage the testicles, leading to problems such as reduced sperm count (oligozoospermia), no sperm in the ejaculate (azoospermia), low testosterone (hypogonadism) and sexual dysfunction, especially after episodes of painful erections called priapism. Research suggests that these issues are increasingly recognised but are still under‑reported [1].

Because of these risks, doctors recommend that men with sickle cell discuss fertility early. Options that can help include freezing sperm before starting treatments that may further harm sperm production (for example, hydroxyurea or stem‑cell transplant) or, if sperm are not available, specialised procedures such as testicular sperm extraction (TESE) combined with assisted reproduction techniques. Research suggests that many men still achieve pregnancy with their partners using these approaches [1].

If you’re a man with sickle cell and you’re thinking about having children, talk to your haematology team or GP. They can arrange a fertility assessment and discuss the best options for you.
